## How to structure your answer

Use **STAR-L**, but treat the Action as a growth plan with phases:

1. **Diagnose**: what the person was great at and the one or two behaviours holding them back.
2. **Stretch**: the opportunity you created and why it fit the gap.
3. **Coach**: how you gave feedback during the stretch.
4. **Sponsor**: how you made their work visible to people who decide promotions.

Finish with evidence (promotion, a project outcome, feedback from others) and what you learned about coaching.

## Model answer

**Situation.** I was the front-end lead for a media publisher's web platform, with eight engineers across two squads. We were about to split into three squads, and I needed a second lead. Marco was our strongest senior engineer: he wrote the cleanest code on the team and was the go-to person for our rendering pipeline. But in planning he tended to go quiet, and when he disagreed with something he would fix it in his own pull requests rather than raise it.

**Task.** My manager and I agreed I would get Marco ready to lead the new article-experience squad within two quarters, and that I would be honest if he was not ready.

**Action.** I started with a direct conversation. I asked what he thought a lead does. His answer was "makes the hard technical calls". I shared my view that most of the job is making decisions *visible* and getting others to own them. We agreed on two growth areas: raising disagreement early and in writing, and planning work for others instead of absorbing it.

Then I gave him a stretch project that needed both: moving article pages from client-side rendering to streaming server rendering, which touched our ad partners and the SEO team. He wrote the RFC. I reviewed two drafts and pushed him to add a "decisions we need from others" section, which forced conversations he would have avoided.

I handed him the weekly sync with the ads team. For the first two, I attended and stayed silent, then gave feedback afterwards. In one, he agreed to a deadline he privately thought was impossible. We debriefed using a simple rule: if you would not bet your own money on a date, say so in the meeting. The next week he renegotiated the date with data from a spike.

I also deliberately stopped answering rendering questions in Slack and redirected them to him, so the team learned to go to him first.

For sponsorship, I had him present the results at the quarterly engineering review and wrote a promotion packet with quotes from the ads and SEO leads, not just from me.

**Result.** The migration cut p75 Largest Contentful Paint on article pages from 3.4 s to 1.9 s and improved ad viewability by 11%. Marco became lead of the new squad after five months. A year later his squad had the best delivery predictability in the department, and he has since grown one of his own seniors into a lead.

**Learning.** I learned to name the gap in behaviours, not traits. "Be more confident" is useless feedback. "Write down your disagreement before the meeting ends" is something people can practise.

## Why this answer works

- **Specific diagnosis**: two concrete behaviours, not "he needed more leadership".
- **Stretch tied to the gap**: the project forced cross-team negotiation, which was exactly what he needed.
- **Coaching through real moments**: the debrief after a bad commitment shows practical, timely feedback.
- **Removing yourself on purpose**: redirecting questions to him shows the candidate understands that leads create space.
- **Sponsorship, not only mentorship**: the packet with feedback from other teams.
- **Multi-level outcome**: a technical result, a promotion, and the cycle repeating with his own reports.

## Red flags to avoid

- Confusing mentoring with teaching technical skills. Senior engineers rarely need more coding advice to become leads.
- Taking credit for the person's growth ("I made him a lead").
- No concrete opportunity, only conversations.
- A plan with no feedback loop or checkpoints.
- No awareness that some great seniors should not become leads, and that this is a valid outcome.

## Likely follow-up probes

- **"What if Marco had not wanted to lead people?"** Talk about the staff/principal individual-contributor path and how you would have still grown his influence without forcing management work.
- **"What did you do when he struggled?"** Use the deadline example, or another moment, and explain how you balanced letting him fail safely against protecting the project.
- **"How did the rest of the team react to him getting the role?"** Discuss transparency about criteria, and making sure others had their own growth paths.
- **"How do you scale this beyond one person?"** Mention lead-of-the-week rotations for incident or planning duty, RFC ownership for every senior, and a reading group on technical leadership.
- **"How do you tell when someone is ready?"** Others already go to them for decisions, they handle disagreements without escalation, and their projects run without you.
