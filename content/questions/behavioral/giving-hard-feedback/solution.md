## How to structure your answer

Use **STAR**, and in Action show the shape of the conversation itself. A simple model is **Situation, Behaviour, Impact, Request**:

1. **Situation and behaviour**: a specific, observable example.
2. **Impact**: what it did to people, the team or the product.
3. **Listen**: ask for their view before proposing anything.
4. **Request and agreement**: what changes, and how you will follow up.

Pick a case where the stakes were real: a strong performer, a senior peer, or feedback that was initially rejected.

## Model answer

**Situation.** I was the front-end lead on a team building an internal design system used by about a dozen product teams. Our most experienced engineer, Priya (not her real name), was technically excellent and wrote most of the core components. But her code reviews on contributions from product teams were harsh: comments like "this is wrong, read the docs" with no explanation, and she often rewrote contributors' PRs herself instead of guiding them.

**Task.** External contributions had dropped from about eight PRs a month to two, and two product teams had started building their own components rather than contributing, which was the opposite of what the design system needed. A product-team lead had also mentioned to me privately that his engineers felt afraid to submit. As her lead, I had to address it, and I knew she might take it badly because she was proud of the quality bar she kept.

**Action.** I prepared before talking to her. I collected three specific review threads and the contribution numbers so the conversation would be about facts, not hearsay. I did it in our regular one-on-one, not in a special meeting that would feel like a trial.

I started by saying what I valued: "The quality of our components is largely because of you, and I want to keep that bar." Then I was direct: "In these three reviews, the comments said what was wrong but not why or how to fix it, and in two cases you rewrote the PR. Since then, contributions have dropped by three quarters and two teams are forking components. I think the reviews are part of the reason."

Then I asked how she saw it. She said she was overwhelmed: she was reviewing almost every PR alone, and fixing things herself was faster than explaining. That was fair and partly my fault for not spreading review load.

We agreed on two changes. She would use a short review template: what to change, why, and a link to the relevant guideline. I would add a second reviewer rotation and write a contribution guide so she did not have to explain the same things repeatedly. I checked in after two weeks, and I praised a specific review she did well in our team channel.

**Result.** Within two months, external contributions were back to about seven a month, and one of the teams that had forked components migrated back. Priya later ran a workshop on writing accessible components for contributors, which was her idea. She told me in her review later that the conversation was uncomfortable but fair.

## Why this answer works

- **High stakes**: a strong senior engineer and a measurable effect on the organisation.
- **Prepared with evidence**: three concrete threads and contribution numbers.
- **Balanced delivery**: genuine appreciation plus a direct statement of the problem.
- **Listened and owned their part**: the lead recognised the review overload they had allowed.
- **Concrete agreement** and follow-up, including public recognition of improvement.
- **Measured outcome**: contributions recovered and a forked team came back.

## Red flags to avoid

- Waiting months, or delivering the feedback only in a performance review.
- Using vague labels ("you're toxic", "you need a better attitude").
- Delivering hard feedback in public or over chat.
- A "feedback sandwich" so soft the message gets lost.
- Not following up after the conversation.
- Presenting yourself as perfect with no part in the problem.

## Likely follow-up probes

- **"What if she had rejected the feedback?"** Restate the impact, agree on a short experiment, and involve her manager if the behaviour continued.
- **"How do you give feedback to someone more senior than you?"** Ask permission, focus on impact on shared goals, and be curious rather than prescriptive.
- **"How often do you give feedback?"** Small and often, positive and constructive, so hard conversations are not rare events.
- **"How do you know if feedback landed?"** Behaviour change over the following weeks, not agreement in the meeting.
- **"Have you ever got this wrong?"** Have a short example where your delivery hurt trust and what you changed.
