## How to structure your answer

This is part definition, part self-assessment. Use **Define – Evidence – Direction**:

1. **Define** the three roles briefly, in terms of what each is accountable for. Acknowledge that companies vary.
2. **Evidence**: one short story that shows which work energises you and where you have the most impact. A mini-STAR is enough.
3. **Direction**: where you want to be, and how this role fits. Be honest if you are still deciding, and say what would help you decide.

A useful working definition (adapt it to the company's career ladder if they publish one):

| Role | Accountable for | Main lever | Typical scope |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tech lead | Delivery and technical quality of one team | Planning, design reviews, unblocking, hands-on code | One team, a few projects |
| Staff engineer | Technical direction across teams | Strategy docs, architecture, solving the hardest cross-cutting problems | Several teams or a domain |
| Engineering manager | People and team health | Hiring, career growth, performance, team structure | A team of people, their outcomes |

## Model answer

"In most companies I have seen, a tech lead owns *how one team delivers*: planning, design reviews, and quality, while still coding a fair amount. A staff engineer owns *technical direction across teams*: the problems that no single team can solve, like a platform migration or a performance strategy. An engineering manager owns *people*: hiring, growth, performance conversations and team structure. All three lead, but the main thing each is judged on is different.

I have done all three to some degree. For the last two years I have been tech lead for a team of six on a SaaS scheduling product. Last year our manager was on parental leave for four months, so I covered most of the management work: one-to-ones, a performance review cycle and two hires. I did it reasonably well. Both hires are doing great, but I noticed my energy dropped when my week was all meetings.

In the same year, I noticed that three teams were each building their own date and time picker, with different time zone bugs. I wrote a short proposal for a shared scheduling component package, got buy-in from the other two leads, and built the core with one engineer from each team. It replaced three implementations, removed about 9,000 lines of code, and time zone bug reports dropped by around 70% over the next two quarters. That was the most satisfying work of my year: technical, cross-team, and multiplying other people's work.

So my direction is the staff track. In three years I would like to be setting front-end direction across a group of teams, with enough time to write code for the hardest parts. This role appeals to me because it describes owning the front-end architecture across squads and partnering with the managers rather than replacing them. I am comfortable doing people work when needed, and I care about mentoring, but I do not want a role where hiring and performance management are the core of the job."

## Why this answer works

- **Clear, accurate definitions** framed by accountability, with an acknowledgement that companies vary.
- **Evidence-based choice**: the candidate tried management and has data about their own energy and impact.
- **A strong staff-style story**: cross-team problem, proposal, buy-in, quantified result.
- **Not dismissive of management**: shows respect for the role and willingness to cover it.
- **Ties directly to the role being hired for**.

## Red flags to avoid

- Treating management as a "promotion" from engineering, or staff as a consolation prize.
- "I want to be a manager so I can make the decisions." Managers mostly do not make technical decisions on strong teams.
- "I want to be staff so I do not have to deal with people." Staff work is mostly about influencing people.
- No evidence: a preference based on title or salary bands.
- A preference that contradicts the role. If the role is 50% people management and you say you never want to manage, either the role is wrong for you or you should say that clearly.

## Likely follow-up probes

- **"How much of your week would you expect to code?"** Give an honest range (for example 30–50% as a lead, less as staff) and say what kind of code: critical paths, prototypes, unblocking, not feature tickets on the critical path that would block the team if you were pulled into meetings.
- **"How would you work with the engineering manager?"** Talk about splitting responsibilities explicitly: you lead technical direction and quality, they lead people and careers, and you share input on performance and hiring.
- **"What if we needed you to manage people for a year?"** Be honest about what you could do well, what support you would need, and whether that would change your interest in the role.
- **"What is the hardest part of the staff role?"** Influence without authority, choosing the right problems, and showing impact when your work is spread across teams.
- **"Would you ever switch tracks?"** Many people move back and forth. Say what would make you consider it.
