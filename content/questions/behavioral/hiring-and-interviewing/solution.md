## How to structure your answer

Use **STAR-L**. Choose a story about **improving the system** of hiring, and keep a specific hiring decision ready as a follow-up.

1. **Situation**: what was wrong with the existing process, measured (pass-through rates, time to hire, early attrition, candidate feedback).
2. **Task**: your role, such as hiring manager partner, loop owner or bar raiser.
3. **Action**: what you changed in the loop, rubrics, interviewer training and decision meeting.
4. **Result**: hiring outcomes and quality of hires 6–12 months later.
5. **Learning**: what you still find hard about hiring.

## Model answer

**Situation.** At a health-tech startup, our front-end team was growing from 6 to 14 engineers in a year. The existing interview was a two-hour take-home React app followed by a whiteboard algorithms round. Our offer acceptance rate was 45%, candidate feedback called the take-home "a weekend of unpaid work", and two of the last five senior hires struggled with the actual job: they were strong at algorithms but weak at accessibility, state design and working with designers.

**Task.** As the front-end lead, I owned the front-end interview loop together with our engineering manager and recruiter.

**Action.** I started by writing down what our senior engineers actually did: building accessible forms, designing component APIs, debugging performance, reviewing code and collaborating with product. Then I redesigned the loop to match.

We replaced the take-home with a 75-minute live pairing session in a prepared repo: extend a small component, fix an accessibility bug and add a test. Candidates could use their own editor and search the web, like real work. We replaced the algorithms round with a front-end system design round and added a code review exercise, where candidates reviewed a realistic PR with planted issues. The behavioural round focused on collaboration and ownership.

Each round got a written rubric with specific signals per level, so "strong senior" meant the same thing to every interviewer. I trained eight interviewers through shadowing and reverse-shadowing, and ran a monthly calibration where we compared scores on the same recorded mock interview.

In debriefs, interviewers submitted written feedback before discussing, to avoid anchoring on the most senior voice. I also introduced the rule that "no hire" feedback had to cite rubric evidence, not "culture fit".

**Result.** Over the next 12 months we hired nine front-end engineers. Offer acceptance rose from 45% to 78%, time from first interview to offer fell from 24 days to 11, and candidate survey scores went from 3.1 to 4.5 out of 5. More importantly, all nine were rated "meets" or above at their six-month review, and the share of women in the front-end team went from one in six to four in fourteen, which I attribute partly to structured rubrics.

**Learning.** I once pushed to hire a candidate with outstanding technical scores despite weak collaboration signals, and it did not work out. I now treat a clear weakness in a core competency as a no, regardless of other strengths.

## Why this answer works

- **Starts from the job**: the loop was redesigned around what engineers actually do.
- **Uses data**: acceptance rate, time to offer, candidate feedback and six-month performance.
- **Fairness built into the process**: rubrics, calibration, independent written feedback.
- **Scales through others**: training eight interviewers instead of interviewing everyone personally.
- **Measures quality of hire**, not just speed.
- **Owns a mistake** honestly and shows how it changed their judgement.

## Red flags to avoid

- "I trust my gut" or "I can tell in five minutes if someone is good."
- Hiring for "culture fit" without defining it, which often hides bias.
- Long unpaid take-homes or trick questions presented as rigour.
- Never having said no to a candidate others liked, or never having changed your mind in a debrief.
- Only talking about speed of hiring, not quality.
- Disparaging candidates you rejected.

## Likely follow-up probes

- **"How do you evaluate senior vs lead candidates differently?"** Leads should show scope across teams, influence and growing others, not just stronger coding.
- **"What did you do when two interviewers disagreed?"** Compare evidence against the rubric, identify missing signal, and if needed run a short follow-up interview rather than defaulting to yes or no.
- **"How do you handle AI tools in coding interviews?"** Be thoughtful: either allow them and assess how candidates verify output, or set clear rules, and design exercises that test reasoning.
- **"How do you sell a role to a strong candidate?"** Talk about real problems, growth paths and honest answers about challenges.
- **"How do you keep interviewers from burning out?"** A rotation, limits per week, and recognising interviewing in performance reviews.
