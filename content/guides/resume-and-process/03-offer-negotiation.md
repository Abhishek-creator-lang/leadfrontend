Negotiation is often the highest-paid hour of your career. Even a modest improvement to an offer compounds through future raises and refreshes. Yet many engineers accept the first number, out of worry about seeming greedy or losing the offer. Professional, well-researched negotiation is **expected** at most tech companies, and offers are almost never withdrawn because of a polite counter.

## Understand the components

| Component | Description | Negotiability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Base salary | Fixed annual pay | Medium; constrained by level bands |
| Annual bonus | Target % of base, performance-dependent | Low (target %), sometimes guaranteed first year |
| Equity (RSUs) | Public company stock vesting over time (often 4 years) | High at many companies |
| Equity (options) | Startup right to buy shares at strike price | Medium; know the details (see below) |
| Sign-on bonus | One-time cash, sometimes split over 1–2 years | High; used to bridge gaps |
| Level | Determines bands for everything above | Medium; requires evidence |
| Start date, remote/hybrid, title, team | Non-monetary terms | Often flexible |
| Benefits, learning budget, relocation | Standardized but sometimes adjustable | Low–medium |

Compare offers on **total compensation (TC)**: base + expected bonus + annualized equity + (sign-on amortized over the first year or two).

### Vesting schedules vary

- Even: 25% per year over 4 years (common)
- Back-loaded: e.g. 5/15/40/40 (Amazon historically, balanced by sign-on cash)
- Front-loaded: higher percentage in year 1 (some companies)
- Cliffs: often a 1-year cliff before the first vest at startups and some public companies

Model year-by-year TC, not just the four-year average. Also ask about **refresh grants**. They drive long-term comp at many public companies.

## Do your research

- **Market data:** levels.fyi, Blind discussions, peers, recruiters you trust, and published pay ranges (required in job postings in many US states and some other jurisdictions).
- **Level bands:** ask the recruiter for the range for your level. Many will share it.
- **Your current TC:** know it precisely, including unvested equity you'd forfeit.
- **Your walk-away number:** decide in advance the minimum you'd accept, and the number that would make you say yes immediately.

## Sources of leverage

| Leverage | Strength |
| --- | --- |
| Competing offers (especially from peer companies) | Strongest |
| Being in late stages elsewhere | Moderate |
| Strong interview feedback / unique skills | Moderate |
| Current comp + unvested equity you'd forfeit | Moderate (justifies sign-on) |
| Willingness to walk away | Underlying everything |

Never invent offers. Recruiters can ask for proof, and the industry is small.

## The process, step by step

### 1. Early stage: salary expectations

Avoid anchoring yourself before you know the level and scope.

> "I'd like to learn more about the role and level first. I'm confident we can find a number that works if it's the right fit. Could you share the range for this position?"

If pressed (and where legal), give a researched range whose bottom is a number you'd be happy with, and phrase it as total comp:

> "Based on my research for staff-level front end roles, I'm targeting total compensation in the range of X to Y."

Many jurisdictions prohibit employers from asking about your salary history. You can politely decline to share it.

### 2. Receiving the offer

- Thank them and express genuine enthusiasm.
- **Don't accept or negotiate on the spot.** Ask for the full details in writing.
- Ask clarifying questions: vesting, refreshers, bonus history, sign-on clawback terms, start date flexibility.

> "Thank you, I'm really excited about this team. Could you send the full details in writing? I'd like to review them and come back to you by Thursday."

### 3. Making the counter

Be specific, justified, and collaborative. Ask for a concrete number, slightly above your target.

> "I'm very excited about the role, and I'd love to make this work. I have a competing offer at total comp around X, and given the scope we discussed, leading the design system and performance work across four teams, I was hoping we could get closer to Y. Specifically, could you look at increasing the equity grant? If we can get there, I'm ready to sign."

Key elements:
- Enthusiasm and intent to join
- The justification (competing offer, scope, forfeited equity)
- A specific number or component
- A signal that agreement leads to a decision ("ready to sign")

### 4. Handling responses

| Response | Your move |
| --- | --- |
| "This is our best offer." | Ask about other components: "I understand base is fixed. Is there flexibility on sign-on or equity?" |
| "Base is at the top of the band." | Ask about level: "Would the loop feedback support the next level?" or sign-on |
| Partial increase | Thank them; decide whether to make one more targeted ask or accept |
| Exploding deadline | Ask politely for more time: "I want to give this the consideration it deserves. Could we extend to Monday?" Most will. |

Usually one or two rounds is appropriate. Endless back-and-forth hurts goodwill.

### 5. Accepting

Get the final offer in writing, confirm all agreed changes, and then accept. Withdraw from other processes courteously. You may meet those people again.

## Evaluating startup equity

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Number of shares **and** fully diluted share count | Gives your ownership % |
| Strike price and latest 409A valuation (US) | Cost to exercise; tax implications |
| Latest preferred price and valuation | Paper value (optimistic) |
| Liquidation preferences | Investors get paid first in exits; can wipe out common shares |
| Vesting and cliff; acceleration on acquisition | When you actually own it |
| Exercise window after leaving (90 days vs extended) | Whether you can afford to keep options |
| ISO vs NSO (US) | Tax treatment |
| Runway and funding plans | Risk of dilution or failure |

A common conservative approach: value startup equity at **zero to a fraction of its paper value** when comparing offers, and treat any upside as a bonus. Ask for more cash if you need financial certainty.

## Non-salary terms worth negotiating

- **Level:** sets the trajectory for all future comp. Worth more than a one-time bump.
- **Sign-on bonus:** to offset unvested equity or a bonus you'd forfeit.
- **Start date:** time off between jobs is valuable and usually free to ask for.
- **Remote/hybrid arrangement:** get it in writing.
- **Title** (especially at startups): "Staff Engineer" or "Front End Lead" if the scope warrants it.
- **Team choice:** in team-match processes.
- **Learning budget, conference travel, equipment.**
- **Relocation package.**

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Better approach |
| --- | --- |
| Accepting on the call | Ask for time and the written offer |
| Negotiating only base | Consider equity, sign-on, level |
| Ultimatums or aggressive tone | Collaborative, enthusiastic framing |
| Lying about competing offers | Use real leverage or justify with scope and market data |
| Negotiating after accepting | Negotiate before acceptance |
| Ignoring vesting and refreshers | Model year-by-year TC |
| Comparing on base alone | Compare TC and growth trajectory |

## Script summary

- **Expectations:** "I'd like to understand the level first. What's the range?"
- **Receiving:** "Thank you, I'm excited. Can you send the details? I'll respond by [date]."
- **Counter:** "I'd love to join. Given [competing offer / scope / forfeited equity], could we get to [number], ideally through [component]? If so, I'm ready to sign."
- **Pushback:** "I understand. Is there flexibility on [other component]?"
- **Deadline:** "Could we extend to [date] so I can make a considered decision?"
